What a screen shows is not what decides what people do with it. So I built a way to measure the gap.

A six-year experimental program at UBC. There was no established measure for the question I wanted to answer, so the first job was building one and proving it worked. Then I extended it in three directions: trust, display complexity, and whether users can judge their own accuracy.

Role: Program lead Org: Brain, Attention & Reality Lab, UBC Span: 2019 - 2025 Output: 3 published papers, 1 under revision
10+ studies. 1,600+ participants. $215K in competitive funding. One question underneath all of it.

The question

Every interface assumes users read what is on it and decide accordingly. But attention runs in two directions at once. What a user looks at takes information in - and wherever their activity is visible to anyone else, it also gives information away. Anyone who has hesitated before opening a message because the sender would see the read receipt has felt both halves at the same time.

How people manage that trade-off, and what it does to the decisions they make, had never been measured. That is the gap I spent six years building instruments for.

Building the measure

With no established method, the hard part was not running studies. It was deciding what would count as evidence at all.

I designed a two-person search-and-signal task. One user navigates a display and selects a target while their gaze is recorded. A second user then watches that gaze replayed over the same display and infers which element was chosen. That captures both halves of the signal in a single design - and, more importantly, it makes the measure falsifiable. If the second user could not beat chance, then the first user's attention carried no signal and the whole approach fails.

They beat chance. And their accuracy moved with the conditions I had manipulated, which is what convinced me the measure was real before I trusted anything it produced. Only then did I rebuild the task in Python to run online, so every lab finding could be re-checked at roughly ten times the sample. Studies were randomized and within-user, modelled with mixed-effects regression across 100,000+ observations.

What it showed

How a user reads a screen is not a fixed property of the screen. It shifts with who can see them, what they believe about the source, and how much is in front of them - and each of those turned out to be measurable.

Trust moved decisions more than the information did

I manipulated only what users believed about the intent of whoever was signalling to them: cooperative, or deceptive. Not the accuracy of the signal, not its salience, just the belief about who was behind it. That alone moved decision rates by about 30 percentage points - a larger effect than the information itself carried.

The same cue moved high-trust and low-trust users in visibly different directions (right, below). A replication segmented by sub-clinical neurodivergent traits showed the effect is not uniform across a user base: the same interface signal lands differently for different segments.

Complexity collapses performance at a threshold, not along a slope

The methodological contribution here matters more than the headline. Everyone knows dense displays hurt performance. What was not known is the shape of the curve. It is not a gentle slope: past a threshold, task success falls off a cliff, and gaze patterns change in kind rather than in degree at the same point (left, below).

In the condition shown, search accuracy went from 82% to 20% against a chance floor of 1 in 9. That is the difference between a heuristic ("avoid clutter") and a criterion a design team can defend a decision with.

Users can judge their own accuracy - but only sometimes

The last extension asked whether self-report is worth anything. Users' confidence did predict whether they were actually right. But the accuracy of that self-assessment was not constant: it was strongest when users were cooperating with the signaller and when a visual aid made the signal easier to send, and it degraded when the goal was adversarial or the display was uniform.

That is a more useful result than a blanket "users don't know what they know." Self-report is a valid signal in some contexts and not others, and the model tells you which context you are in.

Methodology

Program
10+ end-to-end studies, 2019 - 2025, N=1,600+
Design
Randomized, within-user; a two-person search-and-signal task built from scratch
Lab methods
Binocular and mobile eye-tracking, in-person moderated sessions
Scale methods
Task rebuilt in Python for online delivery; behavioural telemetry at roughly 10× lab sample
Analysis
Mixed-effects regression across 100,000+ observations; power analysis to right-size samples; reproducible R and Python pipelines under version control
Funding
$215K+ competitive, including NSERC CGS-D and CGS-M
Output
3 peer-reviewed papers published, 1 under revision; open data and code

What it changes in a product

FindingWhat a product team can do with it
Belief about a source moves decisions ~30 points, more than the content Credibility signals, source badges, and ranking position can outweigh the thing they label. Products rarely measure this, and it differs across user segments.
Complexity collapses performance at a threshold Locating the threshold for your own surface turns "reduce clutter" into a defensible line - a criterion a design review can actually use.
Visibility of one's own activity changes behaviour independently of salience In collaborative, presence-aware, or social products, users respond to what they think others can see about them, not only to what is on screen.
Confidence predicts accuracy, conditionally Self-reported confidence is a usable metric where the model says it holds, and a misleading one elsewhere. Knowing the difference is the finding.

The honest version

None of this shipped into a product. It is academic work, and pretending otherwise would be the wrong way to read it.

What it demonstrates is a capability, and it is the one I would lead with. Most questions arrive with an established instrument attached. The questions that actually decide product direction often do not. I am comfortable in that territory: taking an unmeasured question, designing and validating a measure for it, stating in advance what would prove it wrong, running it at both lab and telemetry scale, and returning an answer specific enough to design against.
